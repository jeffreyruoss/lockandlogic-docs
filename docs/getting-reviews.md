# Getting Google Reviews

> Reviews are the single highest-value thing you can do this month. Here's what works, in order.

---

## Why now

A brand-new Google listing has no track record. Reviews are what turn it from "some place that just opened" into "the escape room in Pottstown" — for Google's ranking, and for the person deciding where to spend Friday night.

They matter more right now than anything technical we could do to the website. A handful of reviews in the first weeks is worth more than the same handful six months from now, because they're what gets you shown to people who have never heard of you.

The good news is you already have the hard part: people walk out of your building happy. Almost nobody asks them.

---

## 1. Ask out loud. This is the whole game.

**A game master asking beats every other method combined.** A card sitting on the counter gets a fraction of the response that a real person asking gets, because the ask is what turns a satisfied customer into a reviewer.

The moment is the two minutes right after a game ends, while the group is still celebrating and their phones are already out for photos. Not at booking, not by email a week later — right then.

Something like:

> "Glad you had fun! If you've got ten seconds, a Google review genuinely helps a new place like us — just scan this."

That's it. Say it every single time, to every group, win or lose. The teams that lost with three seconds left are often the warmest.

::: tip Make it a step, not a favour
Build it into how a game ends — reset the room, walk the group out, take the team photo, ask for the review. If it's a step in the routine it happens every time; if it's something to remember, it won't.
:::

---

## 2. Put the code where the ask happens

The QR code is on the [QR Code Campaigns](/qr-campaigns) page, ready to print. It opens the review box directly — no searching for the business.

Good places for it:

- **A small card handed over with the team photo** — the strongest one, because it rides along with something they want.
- **A table tent at the front desk** where groups settle up.
- **A sticker by the exit**, at eye level.

Print it at 1 inch or larger. The card supports the ask; it doesn't replace it.

---

## 3. Automate the follow-up

Not everyone will do it on the spot. A short email after their game catches a good share of the rest, and once it's set up it runs forever without anyone remembering to do anything.

This sends automatically after each booking with the review link in it. Your developer can set this up in your booking system — ask if it isn't running yet.

---

## 4. Use the team photo

If you take a victory photo, send it afterwards with the review link alongside it. People genuinely want the photo, so the message gets opened, and the ask arrives attached to something you're giving them rather than something you're requesting.

This is the highest-converting trick in the industry, and it costs nothing but a photo you're already taking.

---

## 5. Reply to every review

Reply to all of them, good and bad. It takes a minute, Google takes owner engagement as a signal the business is active, and future customers read the replies as closely as the reviews.

For a good one, be specific and brief — "So glad Grandma Betty's got you! Come back for The Initiation." For a critical one, thank them, own whatever went wrong, and say what you changed. A calm reply to a bad review often reads better to a stranger than the review itself.

---

## What not to do

::: danger Never offer anything in exchange for a review
No discounts, free rooms, raffle entries, or "leave us a review and get $5 off." Google prohibits incentivised reviews outright, and the consequences land on you: reviews get deleted, and the profile can be penalised. Asking everyone is fine. Paying for it in any form is not.
:::

**Don't screen for happy customers.** Asking only the groups who escaped, or asking how their visit was and steering only the positive answers toward Google, is called review gating and it also violates Google's policy. Ask everyone the same way.

**Don't write them yourself, and don't ask staff, friends, or family to.** Google is good at spotting reviews from connected accounts, and the penalty falls on the business. A profile that gets caught can lose every review it has.

**Don't buy them.** Same outcome, faster.

---

## What good looks like

There's no magic number, but useful markers for a new escape room:

| Milestone | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **First 10** | The listing stops looking brand new |
| **25–50** | You're credible against established competitors |
| **Steady trickle** | Recency counts — a review this month beats five from last year |

For scale: the nearest large competitors in King of Prussia have several thousand reviews each. You will not catch them this year, and you don't need to. You need enough that someone searching "escape room Pottstown" sees a real business with real customers — and being the local option is an advantage those chains can't copy.

If every game master asks every group, the rest takes care of itself.
